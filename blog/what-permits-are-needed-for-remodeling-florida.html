What Permits Are Needed for Remodeling in Florida?

August 2, 2026

A kitchen can look like a finish project until the plan moves a wall, adds a circuit, relocates plumbing, or changes an opening. That is when homeowners start asking, what permits are needed for remodeling? In Bradenton, Sarasota, Lakewood Ranch, Anna Maria Island, and nearby Gulf Coast communities, the answer depends on the scope, the property location, and the local building department with jurisdiction over the address.

Permits are not paperwork for paperwork’s sake. They create a documented review and inspection process for work that affects structural integrity, life safety, drainage, energy performance, and building systems. A properly permitted project helps protect the property, supports resale, and gives the owner a clearer record of what was built behind the finished walls.

What Permits Are Needed for Remodeling in Florida?

Most remodeling permits fall into a few practical categories: building, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, roofing, and sometimes site or zoning review. One project may require several trade permits, even when the homeowner sees it as a single renovation.

A building permit commonly applies when a remodel changes framing, load-bearing walls, windows or exterior doors, room layouts, stairs, decks, or structural components. It can also apply to additions, garage conversions, major interior renovations, and work that changes the approved use of a space.

Electrical permits generally cover new circuits, panel upgrades, rewiring, service changes, hardwired equipment, and major lighting changes. Plumbing permits often apply to moving supply or drain lines, adding fixtures, replacing water heaters, or reconfiguring bathrooms and kitchens. Mechanical permits cover new or relocated HVAC equipment, ductwork, exhaust systems, and ventilation work.

The exact permit combination is determined by the work itself, not by the room being remodeled. A cosmetic bathroom update with paint, flooring, a vanity, and like-for-like finish replacements may have a very different permit path than a bathroom renovation that moves a shower drain, installs new ventilation, and alters wall framing.

Projects That Commonly Require a Permit

If the work changes a building system or the building’s structure, assume a permit review may be required until the local authority confirms otherwise. Common examples include:

  • Removing, adding, or modifying walls, especially walls that may carry roof, floor, or ceiling loads
  • Building an addition, enclosed patio, porch, deck, balcony, garage conversion, or accessory structure
  • Replacing windows or exterior doors when openings, wind-load requirements, or egress conditions are involved
  • Installing or relocating plumbing fixtures, water heaters, gas lines, drains, or supply lines
  • Adding circuits, upgrading an electrical panel, rewiring rooms, or installing generators and EV chargers
  • Replacing or modifying HVAC systems, ductwork, kitchen exhaust, bathroom exhaust, or dryer venting
  • Re-roofing, structural repairs, exterior masonry work, concrete slabs, driveways, or significant drainage changes
  • Altering a commercial space through tenant improvements, new partitions, accessibility upgrades, or changes in occupancy

Florida’s coastal conditions make the details matter. Wind resistance, opening protection, flood-zone rules, drainage, elevation, and corrosion-resistant materials can all affect the permit review. A waterfront home or property in a flood-prone area may face requirements that do not apply to a similar project farther inland.

Work That May Not Need a Permit

Many true cosmetic updates can be completed without a building permit. Interior painting, cabinet refacing, replacing finish flooring, installing trim, changing countertops, and similar surface-level improvements are often exempt. Replacing a fixture with a comparable fixture in the same location may also be treated differently than relocating it or changing the connected system.

But “may not need” is not the same as “never needs.” Local exemptions vary, and a simple-looking project can cross into permitted work quickly. For example, replacing cabinets may not require a permit on its own, but a kitchen layout change that adds circuits, moves plumbing, or modifies a load-bearing wall almost certainly requires more review.

The reliable approach is to verify the scope before demolition begins. That protects the schedule and avoids spending money on work that must later be opened, corrected, or redesigned.

Why Jurisdiction Matters in Manatee and Sarasota Counties

Permit requirements are enforced by the local building department that serves the property. Depending on the address, that may be a county department or a municipal jurisdiction such as the City of Bradenton, Sarasota, Palmetto, Holmes Beach, or another local municipality. Each authority can have its own application process, forms, plan-review standards, inspection scheduling procedures, and local amendments.

Homeowners should also separate building permits from other approvals. An HOA or condominium association may require architectural approval before construction starts, but association approval does not replace a government permit. Likewise, a permit does not override deed restrictions, setback requirements, historic district rules, or condominium requirements.

For commercial properties, the review can be broader. A tenant improvement project may require building, electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits, along with accessibility, fire safety, occupancy, and site considerations. A change from one business use to another can trigger additional review even if the physical renovation seems limited.

The Permit Process: What Happens Before and During Construction

A well-managed permit process starts with a clear scope. The contractor evaluates the existing conditions, identifies structural and trade work, and determines which drawings or documents the jurisdiction is likely to require. Smaller projects may need basic plans and product information. Larger renovations, additions, structural alterations, and commercial work may require detailed construction plans, engineering, energy documentation, surveys, or product approvals.

After the permit application is submitted, the building department reviews the documents for code compliance. Review comments are common. They are not necessarily a sign that a project is in trouble. They are the department’s request for clarification or correction before construction proceeds.

Once the permit is issued, required inspections occur at specific stages. Depending on the work, that can include foundation or slab inspection, framing, rough electrical, rough plumbing, rough mechanical, insulation, drywall, roofing, and final inspection. Work should not be covered before the applicable inspection is approved. Closing walls before rough inspections can create expensive delays because the work may need to be exposed again.

Permit timing varies. A straightforward trade permit may move faster than a structural renovation requiring engineered plans and multiple review cycles. Material selections can also affect timing, particularly for windows, exterior doors, roofing, and other products that must meet Florida requirements.

Who Should Pull the Remodeling Permit?

For most substantial remodeling work, the licensed contractor handling the construction should manage the permit process. This helps align the approved plans, trade coordination, inspections, and final closeout under one accountable party. It also gives the owner a clear point of contact when the building department has questions.

Some owners consider pulling a permit themselves to reduce cost or speed up the process. Florida’s owner-builder option exists in certain situations, but it carries real responsibilities. The owner may become responsible for code compliance, scheduling inspections, supervising the work, correcting deficiencies, and managing subcontractors. It is not a shortcut around licensing requirements or construction accountability.

Be cautious if a contractor asks a homeowner to pull a permit for work the contractor will perform. There can be legitimate reasons to discuss permitting roles, but the arrangement should be fully understood before work begins. A professional contractor should be transparent about licensing, insurance, permit responsibility, project documents, and inspection expectations.

The Cost of Skipping a Permit

Unpermitted work can create problems long after construction is finished. During a sale, buyers, inspectors, lenders, and insurers may question improvements that have no permit history or final approval. The owner could face delays, fines, corrective work, or the requirement to obtain an after-the-fact permit.

The immediate risk is just as serious. Without plan review and inspections, framing connections, electrical protection, plumbing venting, ventilation, waterproofing, and wind-resistance details may never be independently checked. Those are not cosmetic concerns. They affect safety, durability, moisture control, and the long-term value of the property.

For Gulf Coast homes, doing the work correctly is especially important. High winds, heavy rain, humidity, flood exposure, and salt air place real demands on exterior systems and structural connections. Good workmanship and code compliance work together to protect the investment.

Plan the Scope Before You Start

The best time to address permits is before selecting finishes, ordering materials, or scheduling demolition. Start with the intended scope, identify what will change behind the walls and outside the building envelope, and confirm the local requirements for the property address. A written estimate should make clear what work is included and how permitting, plans, inspections, and allowances will be handled.

The goal is not to make a renovation feel complicated. It is to build it correctly, document it properly, and deliver finished work that is built to last.

Before the first wall comes down, get the permit question answered for your specific address and scope. That one step can keep a remodeling project moving with fewer surprises and far more confidence.
